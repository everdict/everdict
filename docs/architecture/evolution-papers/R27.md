# R27 — Humanity’s Last Exam: difficulty, calibration, and the evolution of evaluation

Humanity's Last Exam (HLE) is a collection of unusually difficult, closed-ended academic questions. The Nature publication, *A benchmark of expert-level academic questions to assess AI capabilities*, appeared on 28 January 2026. Its contribution is an evaluation instrument and a process for maintaining it, rather than an agent optimizer. For Everdict, HLE connects candidate evolution to three requirements: separate real progress from selection effects, measure confidence as well as accuracy, and version changes to the evaluator itself.[^1]

## Why create another academic benchmark?

When nearly every strong model scores highly on a benchmark, that benchmark loses resolution at the frontier. HLE responds to saturation in general academic tests such as MMLU by recruiting specialists to create questions that contemporary models cannot solve. It combines broad subject coverage, expert construction, multimodal inputs, automated answer comparison, and adversarial difficulty filtering.

The intended construct is expert-level performance on known, verifiable academic questions. It is narrower than autonomous research. Solving a difficult question whose answer is already established does not demonstrate the ability to formulate a research agenda, build apparatus, run experiments, evaluate a novel theory, or manage a long project. HLE's title should not erase that distinction.

Relative to GPQA, it broadens subjects and input formats. Relative to FrontierMath, it includes much more than mathematics. Relative to GDPval and remote-work evaluations, it emphasizes compact answers rather than usable deliverables. Relative to SealQA, it tries to avoid questions answered by simple retrieval instead of deliberately studying noisy search. These instruments test different capabilities and should not be treated as interchangeable rankings.[^2]

## What the dataset contains

The public collection has 2,500 questions spanning over a hundred subjects, grouped into eight categories. Figure 2 allocates 41% to mathematics, 11% to biology/medicine, 10% to computer science/AI, 9% each to physics, humanities/social science, and other subjects, 7% to chemistry, and 4% to engineering. The breadth is real, but the aggregate is strongly shaped by mathematics and STEM.[^3]

About 14% of questions require an image. Approximately 24% are multiple choice with five or more alternatives; the remainder require a short exact answer. Contributors supply the question, answer, rationale, subject, name, and affiliation. Questions must be original or a non-trivial synthesis, self-contained, unambiguous, solvable, and resistant to simple web lookup. Highly specialized terminology is permitted when standard within the field.

Nearly 1,000 contributors from more than 500 institutions in fifty countries participate. A $500,000 prize pool awards $5,000 to the top fifty questions and $500 to the next five hundred; accepted contributors can receive coauthorship. These incentives help recruit expertise but also make acceptance rules and auditing consequential parts of dataset design.

The benchmark allows insights drawn from contributors' own research experience, including knowledge difficult to corroborate through an ordinary literature search. That can challenge memorization, while making external verification harder. A known answer in the submission process is not automatically an independently established answer for every reviewer.

## Difficulty filtering and expert review

Before submission, text-and-image questions are tested on GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and o1. Text-only questions additionally use o1-mini and o1-preview. Exact-answer questions must stump every model; multiple-choice questions can allow one model success to account for lucky guesses. Over 70,000 attempts produce about 13,000 questions forwarded to human review.[^4]

The first review round supplies one to three expert reviews and revisions. Its rubric ranges from discard to top quality, with an unsure option. The second round considers both the question and earlier feedback, after which organizers approve inclusion. The supplementary instructions discourage artificial difficulty from enormous computation, poorly rendered notation, or images that could be text. Fixing formatting can make a model solve a question, so failure alone does not establish advanced reasoning difficulty.

A crucial qualification appears in the Methods and supplement: ordinary reviewers are not expected to fully verify every solution. The first-round guidance estimates three to five minutes per review and permits moving on when solving would require more than five minutes or knowledge the reviewer lacks. The process primarily checks suitability, clarity, and plausibility, followed by deeper audits. Describing it as independently solving every submitted problem would overstate the evidence.

The filtering also makes original models' low accuracy partly a design outcome. Multiple-choice guessing, stochastic inference, and imperfect filtering leave non-zero scores. Small changes near zero need care: they may reflect guessing or sample noise rather than a meaningful capability advance. No single random-guess baseline of 20% applies to the entire dataset, because most questions are not multiple choice and option counts vary.

## Evaluation and the meaning of exact match

A standardized prompt asks for an explanation, final answer, and confidence from 0% to 100%. The main evaluation measures answers without an agentic search-and-tool workflow. An o3-mini-2025-01-31 judge with structured decoding extracts the final answer, compares it to the supplied reference, gives a short justification, and extracts confidence.[^5]

Although the dataset calls a format exact match, the judge allows equivalent representations such as fractions and decimals and small numerical differences. This is semantic answer matching with a model, not literal byte equality. The judge is instructed to compare answers without independently solving the problem or disputing the reference. It can therefore preserve a wrong reference label, and its tolerance policy is part of the score.

If a response omits confidence, the extraction prompt assigns 100. Missing-format behavior can thus affect measured calibration. An evolution experiment should separately count missing confidence and inspect judge errors; otherwise a formatting change may look like a change in epistemic reliability.

## Accuracy and calibration results

| Model | Main reported accuracy | RMS calibration error |
|---|---:|---:|
| GPT-4o | 2.7% ± 0.6 | 89% |
| Claude 3.5 Sonnet | 4.1% ± 0.8 | 84% |
| Gemini 1.5 Pro | 4.6% ± 0.8 | 88% |
| o1 | 8.0% ± 1.1 | 83% |
| DeepSeek R1, text-only subset | 8.5% ± 1.2 | 73% |
| Claude 4 Sonnet, post-release | 7.8% ± 1.1 | 75% |
| Gemini 2.5 Pro, post-release | 21.6% ± 1.6 | 72% |
| GPT-5, post-release | 25.3% ± 1.7 | 50% |

These are the publication's historical Table 1 results, not a current leaderboard. The table prints uncertainty margins without fully specifying their calculation in that table's caption. Text-only DeepSeek is evaluated on a different subset. Models released after publication of the dataset have a different opportunity for exposure, which is why the authors label them separately.[^6]

Calibration asks whether claimed confidence agrees with observed correctness. A model assigning 80% confidence to a group should answer roughly 80% of that group correctly. RMS calibration error summarizes squared gaps between confidence and observed accuracy across groups, followed by a square root. It is not the fraction of answers that are false, nor a direct guarantee about any individual answer.

The large errors show that these systems often fail while claiming high confidence. Better accuracy and better calibration are distinct objectives: in the text-only extended table, o3 has 20.6% accuracy and 36% calibration error, while GPT-5 has 26.3% and 50%. The more accurate model is not necessarily the better calibrated one. Bin construction, sample size, confidence elicitation, and extraction defaults should remain fixed when comparing candidates.

## Domain, modality, and answer-format breakdowns

The extended tables separate text-only, multimodal-only, exact-answer, and multiple-choice scores. GPT-4o obtains 1.8% on exact answers and 5.6% on multiple choice; o1 obtains 6.7% and 12.0%; Gemini 2.5 Pro obtains 19.6% and 28.1%. Choice options can supply clues and permit lucky success. These are different question subsets, so the difference is not a controlled experiment changing the format of the same questions.[^7]

Multimodal-only results include GPT-4o 5.3%, o1 9.4%, and Gemini 2.5 Pro 19.0%. Their comparison with text-only scores also mixes content difficulty with modality. It cannot isolate the cost of visual understanding without matched representations.

Domain breakdowns expose strengths hidden by an aggregate. For example, in the text-only table, o3-mini scores 18.6% in mathematics but 5.2% in humanities; DeepSeek-R1 scores 9.1% and 10.4% respectively. The benchmark-wide score heavily weights mathematics, so a broadly useful evolution policy should also report domain-level regressions and task counts.

## Reasoning-token analysis is observational

The paper bins completed responses by generated output length, including reasoning tokens, on a base-two scale. Accuracy rises approximately log-linearly until around 2^14, or 16,384, tokens, then declines.[^8]

This is not a randomized experiment assigning the same question different token budgets. Actual output length is influenced by question difficulty, model behavior, and whether a model becomes stuck. Long unsuccessful traces can therefore cause the observed pattern without extra tokens being intrinsically harmful. The result motivates a controlled budget study; it does not establish 16,384 as an optimal universal limit.

For Everdict, record both allowed budget and actual consumption. Compare the same task families under declared budget conditions and include unsuccessful runs. Selecting only successful traces or treating observed length as an externally assigned intervention would confound the conclusion.

## Audits, disputed answers, and the rolling dataset

Two pre-release audits each ask qualified students to fully solve a sample of 200 questions. The first identifies patterns of imprecision and guides revisions; a second sample checks the revised process. The paper reports a final estimated expert disagreement rate of 15.4% for the public set. This is a disagreement estimate, not proof that exactly 15.4% of all labels are objectively wrong.[^9]

The authors also discuss a targeted biology, chemistry, and health review: approximately 18% disagreement under their multi-reviewer reconciliation, versus about 25% if any single dissenter is sufficient. This methodological explanation matters, but does not eliminate the underlying uncertainty. Consensus, an author's rebuttal, and an independently verified solution are different forms of evidence.

After release, a bug bounty accepts label and question-error reports for manual resolution. Search-augmented models identify potentially searchable items, which humans audit and remove when a simple search suffices. Late contributions create another private set. HLE-Rolling is described as a dynamic fork for corrections and new questions; the paper's stated plan should not be confused with a fixed version of the original evaluation.

The public/private split supports overfitting checks, but a private set alone cannot show that every reported public improvement generalizes. Similarly, the audits are dataset-quality studies, not a broad human exam baseline: experts solving selected questions in their specialties do not establish one human score across all subjects.

## A concrete Everdict evolution design

Use HLE-like questions to test a candidate that adds uncertainty estimation, prerequisite checks, and explicit final-answer validation. Compare the baseline, a static procedure, and a curator-maintained procedure at matched task and inference budgets. Report accuracy, calibration, selective accuracy at several answer-coverage levels, and cost. Lower confidence alone should not earn a better task-success score. This experiment is a proposal, not an HLE result or an implemented Everdict change.

Separate families of closely related questions across train, selection, and confirmation sets. A mathematically equivalent parameter variant can reveal the same solution method, so random row splitting is insufficient. Retain a private confirmation pool and record each disclosure used during evolution.

Treat evaluator corrections as versioned changes with their own evidence. If a reference is disputed, preserve the original judgment, record the dispute and resolution, and rerun both baseline and candidate against the same corrected version. Never improve a candidate's apparent history by quietly replacing only its failed labels. A curator may recommend a correction; the confirmation authority must remain separate. This is the central evolution lesson: the agent and the benchmark can both improve, but their changes must remain distinguishable.
