# R25 — GDPval: evaluating professional artifacts and the cost of review

GDPval asks whether an AI system can produce a work deliverable that a qualified professional prefers to a human reference. It extends evaluation beyond answers and code into presentations, spreadsheets, documents, designs, and other occupational artifacts. For Everdict, its strongest lesson is that an evolution loop must measure usable results and the human work needed to obtain them. Fast generation alone is a poor proxy. This note follows arXiv 2510.04374v1, including the cost model and grading appendices.[^1]

## Problem and relation to neighboring benchmarks

Question answering measures knowledge, coding tests measure executable behavior, and browsing benchmarks measure information gathering. Professional work often combines these capabilities with interpretation, presentation, and adherence to a request. A spreadsheet can calculate correctly yet be difficult to inspect; an attractive presentation can contain unsupported claims. GDPval evaluates the final artifact as a whole through occupational experts.

Its task unit is a self-contained request with necessary reference files and a corresponding human deliverable. This differs from observing an employee throughout a project: the benchmark largely supplies context upfront, omits much tacit organizational knowledge, and does not model negotiation, collaboration, or changing requirements. The tasks are economically motivated samples, not a representative estimate of all work or the proportion of jobs that can be automated.[^2]

## How occupations and tasks were selected

The full collection contains 1,320 tasks across 44 occupations: 30 per occupation. The public gold subset contains 220 tasks, five per occupation. Nine US sectors are selected using their shares of GDP in the second quarter of 2024. Within sectors, the authors prioritize occupations with large aggregate wages and predominantly digital tasks; retail contributes four qualifying occupations, producing 44 rather than 45.[^2]

Aggregate wages combine employment and annual pay, with an hourly-to-annual conversion where necessary. This is a selection proxy: wages are not identical to value added. The procedure uses BLS occupational data, industry employment distributions, and O*NET task descriptions. GPT-4o classifies whether tasks are digitally executable; task relevance, importance, and frequency weight the occupation-level estimate, and a 60% digital threshold is applied. Comparisons against established cognitive/manual task indices check consistency, rather than causally validate automation potential.

Occupational experts need at least four years of experience; the participating group averages fourteen. Recruitment includes screening, interviews, and training, with fewer than 10% of applicants accepted. At least five qualified professionals participate per occupation. Authors adapt real work while removing sensitive information and contribute requests, reference materials, and completed human outputs.

Quality assurance combines model-assisted screening with human decisions. Tasks receive at least three human reviews and about five on average, including generalist and occupational checks and revision. This provides stronger construct grounding than model-generated requests alone, while still introducing expert-selection and review-policy choices.

## Coverage and unresolved descriptive statistics

In the gold set, 67.7% of tasks include a reference file. Mapped O*NET coverage includes 25 of 35 skills, 26 of 41 work activities, and 208 of 1,470 occupation-specific tasks. The last proportion is only about 14.15%; broad coverage of general skills should not be confused with exhaustive coverage of professional activities. Reviewers classify 89.07% of gold requests as well specified, 8.28% as underspecified, and 2.66% as overspecified.[^3]

Some reported statistics do not align across sections. The introduction describes roughly seven hours of human completion time. The cost appendix uses a gold-set mean of 404 minutes, approximately 6.73 hours, and $361; another descriptive table reports gold-set means of 9.49 hours and $398.46. The full-set table reports 8.63 hours. These should not be silently combined into one consistent population estimate: the paper does not resolve the differing values in those sections. The note uses the cost appendix's inputs only when explaining its cost calculation.

## The evaluation protocol and what a win means

The main comparison includes GPT-4o, o4-mini, o3, GPT-5, Claude Opus 4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Grok 4. For each model and task, three deliverables are sampled. Each is compared against the human reference by three different human graders, yielding nine comparisons per task/model. The resulting 1,980 votes per model are clustered within 220 tasks, not 1,980 independent work assignments.[^4]

Graders see the request, reference files, and two anonymized deliverables and choose the better output or a tie. Anonymization reduces explicit identity effects, but stylistic patterns or occasional model self-identification can reveal origin. File rendering and inspection also affect judgments.

Harnesses matter. OpenAI models have web access and a code execution environment with substantial document-processing support. Claude uses an interface with upgraded file-creation capabilities. Consequently, results compare deployed model-plus-tool systems. They do not isolate the language model while keeping artifact-generation capabilities identical.

Claude Opus 4.1 wins or ties in 47.6% of comparisons against human work. This is neither a strict-win rate nor a claim that 47.6% of jobs are replaceable. The paper also discusses task-level aggregation, which has a different denominator. GPT-5 is comparatively strong on accuracy and instruction following, while Claude's visual presentation helps on PDFs, spreadsheets, and slides. Task composition therefore influences the overall ranking.[^4]

## Reasoning, prompting, and best-of-four selection

For o3 and GPT-5, increasing reasoning effort improves reported performance across low, medium, and high settings. This is evidence within the tested systems; it does not imply unlimited improvement with additional inference time.

The authors investigate instructions that require page or slide inspection, legible fonts, correct file placement, and checks for layout problems. Reported process measurements include visual inspection rising from 15% to 97%, disappearance of the black-square PDF issue that had affected more than half of PDFs, and egregious slide problems falling from 86% to 64%. The formatting prompt improves human preference by about five percentage points.[^5]

A further pipeline samples four candidate deliverables and uses a GPT-5 judge to choose one. The judge receives the task, references, and candidates. The paper also discusses a scaffold with enabled tooling. These interventions address different failure modes: instructions improve generation discipline, tool support improves what can be produced, and best-of-four selects among outputs at added cost. A bundled improvement cannot be attributed entirely to one component. General wording in a prompt is not itself proof that tuning did not overfit the benchmark; a separately frozen evaluation is still needed.

## The cost model: generation is only one term

The cost appendix distinguishes a naive ratio from a workflow that requires human review and fallback. Let H be human completion time, M model generation time, R human review time, and w the probability that a model attempt is accepted under the assumed quality criterion. Trying once has expected time:

`M + R + (1 − w)H`.

Trying at most n times before fallback has expected time:

`(M + R)[1 − (1 − w)^n]/w + (1 − w)^n H`.

The expression assumes identical, independent attempt success at a fixed task; the w=0 case must be handled by its limit. In practice, repeated failures can be highly correlated. Human pairwise preference is also a proxy for acceptance in a real collaborative workflow, rather than a measured production success probability.[^6]

The cost appendix estimates mean review at 109 minutes and $86. Model costs use API measurements for OpenAI systems; comparable measured costs are not provided for all other model families.

| System | Naive speedup | Speedup with one attempt and review | Naive cost reduction factor | Cost reduction factor with one attempt and review |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| GPT-4o | 327× | 0.87× | 5,172× | 0.90× |
| o4-mini | 186× | 1.02× | 1,265× | 1.06× |
| o3 | 161× | 1.08× | 480× | 1.13× |
| GPT-5 | 90× | 1.12× | 474× | 1.18× |

These are scenario calculations from Table 2, not measured organizational productivity gains. For GPT-4o, quality adjustment makes the modeled process slower and more expensive than the human baseline despite spectacular naive ratios. The repeated-attempt scenario reaches 1.39× speed and 1.63× cost reduction for GPT-5, but only 0.46× and 0.53× for GPT-4o. Reviewing unsuccessful outputs can dominate inexpensive generation.

The calculation omits several real effects, including reviewing human work, poor human deliverables, adoption overhead, and the unequal consequences of different errors. It is a useful sensitivity model, not a labor-market forecast.

## Error analysis and context sensitivity

Performance generally decreases for tasks estimated to require longer human effort. This is observational: long tasks differ in complexity and artifact requirements, so the analysis does not isolate duration as the cause.

Expert explanations identify formatting, instruction-following failures, hallucinations, and calculation errors. In a follow-up analysis of a subset of GPT-5 losses, approximately 29% are rated bad or catastrophic, including about 3% catastrophic. That denominator is selected losses, not all model outputs. A new grader actually prefers the model in 23% of these comparisons, illustrating substantial judgment disagreement.[^7]

An under-context experiment shortens requests to 42% of their original token length by removing information about inputs, approach, and deliverable format. GPT-5 performance falls. This uses an earlier gold-set version, so its scores should not be mixed directly with the main comparison. It tests missing information in a one-shot setting, not whether interactive clarification can recover performance or whether concise prompts are inherently worse.

## Automated grading and its measurement limits

An experimental GPT-5-based grader achieves reported agreement of 65.7% with human judgments, against 70.8% human–human agreement. These percentages use an ordinal similarity: `1 − |H − A|`, with each judgment encoded as 0, 0.5, or 1. A win-versus-tie mismatch earns half credit. This is not exact three-class accuracy, Cohen's kappa, or chance-corrected reliability.[^8]

The evaluation excludes twelve of 220 tasks that the grading environment cannot adequately handle, with further invalid judgments filtered. The grader has Python but no internet; software requiring other languages or external resources, font differences, and audio inspection cause problems. Agreement therefore describes an eligible subset and usable grades, not universal automated review capability.

Agreement is lower on outputs from the grader's own model family, consistent with possible self-preference but not conclusive proof of its cause. The authors recommend human grading for reliable comparisons and describe the automated service as experimental. In an evolution loop, optimizing directly against this grader could exploit preferences or rendering limitations that are not shared by professionals.

## A concrete Everdict evolution experiment

The proposed intervention is an artifact-validation skill: inspect generated files, verify requested calculations and content, render visual outputs, and correct material defects. Compare a baseline, the same executor with this skill, and the same skill plus best-of-four selection. Match total budgets where possible and report extra generation and review costs separately. This is a proposed experiment, not an implemented Everdict feature.

Freeze training, selection, and confirmation task families before editing the skill. Record exact artifact bytes, fonts, renderer versions, tool availability, candidate digest, and evaluator identity. A changed renderer or judge changes the evaluation condition, even when the task text is unchanged. Keep correctness, completeness, visual usability, and severe errors separately visible instead of hiding them in one preference score.

Use blinded, order-swapped human comparisons on confirmation tasks and uncertainty estimates clustered by task. Automated critique can guide revisions on allowed development evidence, but should not independently authorize promotion. Confirm whether a cheaper accepted output remains cheaper after review and fallback. Retain rejected candidates and raw grading evidence so that a polished but inaccurate artifact cannot silently rewrite the record of its failure.

## Reading the evidence conservatively

GDPval supplies unusually rich, expert-authored artifact tasks, a human comparison protocol, and a valuable demonstration that review changes economic conclusions. Its limits are one-shot context, selected occupations, heterogeneous harnesses, subjective preferences, incomplete automated grading, and unresolved descriptive statistics. Its practical contribution to evolution research is an evaluation target and accounting discipline, rather than an optimizer or evidence that autonomous professional work is already reliable.
