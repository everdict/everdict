# E11 — POET: Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer

## Reading guide and evidence scope

Wang et al., **1901.01753v3 (2019)**, proposes the **Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer**, which evolves challenges and learns solutions to them in the same run. The full title is *Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer (POET): Endlessly Generating Increasingly Complex and Diverse Learning Environments and Their Solutions*. This note covers the full text, all four algorithms, supplemental details, quantitative figures, and main qualitative cases. It does not include later Enhanced POET results or claim an independent reproduction.[^1]

Unlike the frozen-LLM context methods in this collection, this implementation **optimizes neural controller weights**. Its broader contribution is the organization of search: maintain several environment–solver pairs, create appropriately difficult new environments, and repeatedly test whether a solver learned elsewhere helps. For Everdict, this motivates exploration curricula and transfer experiments while preserving a separate, fixed confirmation contract.

## Background: why a direct curriculum may miss a useful route

A difficult task may offer poor learning signals from a random policy. Making it gradually harder can help, but the most direct sequence of increasingly similar tasks may still miss the necessary behavior. A useful skill might first emerge on a different branch and later transfer back to an easier problem.

[MAP-Elites](E10.md), novelty search, and other quality-diversity methods preserve different kinds of solutions as possible stepping stones. However, their niches or environment are usually fixed. **Minimal Criterion Coevolution (MCC)** evolves problems and solvers together: each must satisfy a basic condition relative to the other population. In a maze example, a solver must solve some maze, and a maze must be solved by some solver. This can diversify the population, but lacks pressure to further optimize a solver once it satisfies the criterion, and may reject a slightly harder problem that no current solver can finish immediately.[^2]

POET adds optimization within each niche and admits environments that appear learnable rather than already fully solved. It also repeatedly attempts **goal switching**, borrowing the idea that expertise in one objective can become a starting point for another. It differs from ordinary self-play because the non-opponent environment changes too. But the paper’s implementation still uses a fixed robot, fixed reward definition, and bounded family of terrains; its title expresses an open-ended ambition, not a demonstrated infinite process.

## The inner optimizer: what evolution strategies actually learn

Each controller is a neural network with **24 inputs, two 40-unit tanh hidden layers, and four outputs** for hip/knee motors. Ten inputs are terrain/LIDAR readings; the remaining fourteen describe body/joint state and contacts. There is no language model, text memory, or self-authored code in this experiment.[^3]

The inner **evolution strategy (ES)** perturbs the weight vector `θ` with Gaussian noise `σε`, evaluates complete episodes, and increases the probability of perturbations associated with higher return. For raw returns, the score-function estimator is:

`gradient ≈ (1 / (nσ)) Σ return(θ + σεᵢ) εᵢ`.

This estimates the gradient of expected return under a smoothed distribution of policies. It does not require differentiating through the environment. The implementation rank-normalizes returns and uses Adam, so its actual update is not simply the raw-return formula above. Each ES step samples **512 parameter vectors**. Learning rate starts at 0.01, decays by 0.9999 per step to a floor of 0.001; noise starts at 0.1, decays by 0.999 to a floor of 0.01. A new pair or accepted transfer resets Adam state and these schedules.

An ES update aims to improve expected performance but is not individually gated by a strict non-regression test. A single stochastic update can reduce measured return. This differs from the explicit destination-environment comparison used to accept a transfer.

## The outer loop: creating environments, improving solvers, transferring

POET starts with one flat environment and a random controller. Each main iteration performs local ES optimization for active pairs; at scheduled intervals it creates environments and attempts transfers. Active population size is capped, with **20 pairs** in the main multi-obstacle study. When capacity is exceeded, the oldest pairs leave active training. Their environment encodings remain in the novelty archive; the algorithm does not specify that every retired policy remains an eligible transfer source.[^4]

Environment creation has several checks:

1. A parent environment may reproduce once its current solver scores at least **200**. This is distinct from the experiment’s full success threshold.
2. Eligible parents are sampled uniformly to generate up to **512 child candidates**. The child terrain mutates obstacle parameters and receives a fresh stored seed. Its initial solver is copied from its parent.
3. The initial pair must satisfy **50 ≤ return ≤ 300**. This minimal criterion rejects candidates that appear too difficult or trivial under the tested controller.
4. The supplement ranks remaining environments by novelty relative to active and previously admitted environments. Novelty is average Euclidean distance to the **five nearest environment encodings**, a genetic/parameter-space measure, not measured behavioral novelty.
5. Candidate solvers from other active environments are tested in the child environment, including one-step adapted proposals. The resulting best pair must pass the minimal criterion again before admission. A per-reproduction admission cap limits resource growth.

The second minimal-criterion check is meaningful: an environment that looks challenging to its parent may be trivial to another solver. The first check can also reject a terrain that a different solver might have handled, so the heuristic does not exhaustively classify learnability.

There is an implementation-description conflict: Section 4.2 says excess eligible children are admitted in **lower-return order**, while Algorithm 3 and its explanation use **novelty order**. Exact mutation/transfer intervals and the admitted-child cap are left as parameters rather than fully enumerated numeric settings in this text. A faithful reproduction needs the implementation configuration; this note does not invent values.

## Transfer: direct reuse and one-step adaptation

For a target environment, POET evaluates every other active solver in two forms: its existing weights and a copy after **one ES step in the target**. The best candidate replaces the target’s incumbent only if its measured target return is higher. Existing-weight replacement is **direct transfer**; replacement after one-step target optimization is **proposal transfer**.[^4]

This separates “already good here” from “a better starting point after limited adaptation.” The source solver need not be modified. Transfer can occur between distant branches or from a child environment back to its parent. It is not crossover or averaging two controllers’ weights. At population size `M`, a full transfer sweep considers many source–target pairs, including costly 512-sample ES proposals; its expense cannot be counted as merely `M` cheap evaluations.

## Environment encoding, reward, and what success means

The 2D bipedal walker can face stump heights, gap widths, stair heights, stair counts, and ground roughness. Heights and gaps are encoded as ranges from which concrete obstacles are sampled. A seed stored with each environment fixes its realization for repeatability. Repeatedly training on that fixed realization is not a test on unseen terrain seeds.[^3]

| Parameter | Initial value | Mutation size | Maximum in encoding table |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stump height range | 0–0.4 | 0.2 | endpoints up to 5 |
| Gap width range | 0–0.8 | 0.4 | endpoints up to 10 |
| Stair height range | 0–0.4 | 0.2 | endpoints up to 5 |
| Stair count | 1 | 1 | 9 |
| Roughness | Uniform 0–0.6 | Uniform 0–0.6 | 10 |

Mutation can increase or decrease parameters; a curriculum branch need not become harder in every coordinate at every step. Some studies enable only selected obstacle types.

Episodes end after at most **2,000 simulation steps**, a fall, or reaching the end. Reward favors forward progress, with posture and torque terms and a **−100 fall penalty**. A course is called solved only when the agent both **reaches the end and scores at least 230**. Score 200 allows reproduction but does not necessarily establish full success. Admission upper bound 300 likewise does not mean a solved controller is later forbidden from exceeding 300; the backward-transfer example reaches 349.

The paper does not evolve reward functions or robot morphology. Those are proposed extensions in the discussion. For Everdict, modifying a training scenario is therefore a closer analogy than allowing the candidate to rewrite the authoritative success test.

## Experiment 1: direct optimization from scratch

POET first produces and solves five illustrative terrain challenges: large gaps, rough ground, high stumps, large stairs, and a mixed gap/stump course. For each selected terrain, the same ES optimizer is run **five times from random initialization**, for up to **16,000 ES steps** per run. The best baseline scores are respectively **17.9, 39.6, 13.6, 24.0, and 19.2**; POET exceeds the 230 success threshold on all five.[^5]

The baseline often learns to advance a little and then stop before an obstacle. Avoiding the fall penalty yields a locally acceptable policy while blocking exploration of a successful jump. The qualitative figures show POET’s learned ways of navigating obstacles that these direct runs do not overcome.

This is evidence of a severe optimization barrier for the tested optimizer and initialization, not proof that no other algorithm can solve the terrain. The targets are selected from problems **POET itself generated and solved**, rather than a prespecified representative test set. The authors compare five ES results with one selected POET score using one-sample t-tests (`p < 0.01`). These tests measure separation from the chosen score; they do not estimate between-run POET uncertainty or remove target-selection bias. The baseline budget is explicit, but this experiment is not a matched accounting of all POET exploration costs.

## Experiment 2: a direct-path curriculum

The stronger control begins on flat ground and gradually increases obstacle parameters toward a selected target. Each parameter has equal chance to stay unchanged or increase by its mutation step until reaching the target value. The agent advances when it reaches the same 200-point reproduction criterion and uses ES throughout. Unlike POET, this produces one monotonic route toward one known target, without lateral or backward transfers.[^6]

Three POET runs last up to **25,200 outer iterations**, with 20 active pairs and 512 samples per ES step. Each takes about **ten days on 256 CPU cores**. An outer iteration includes many pair updates and possibly transfer/proposal work; it is not one environment episode or one ES update.

Difficulty is defined by how many of these conditions a terrain satisfies: **stump upper height ≥2.4, gap upper width ≥6.0, roughness ≥4.5**. One, two, or three satisfied conditions define challenging, very challenging, or extremely challenging. The original Hardcore reference values are 2.0, 3.0, and 1.0. These parameter thresholds describe difficulty for this domain; they are not a general measure of intelligence.

Two solved targets per difficulty level are sampled from each POET run: **18 targets**, with five direct-path attempts each. The control receives the total ES steps in the target’s ancestral chain, accounting for transfers. This is a lineage-based budget, **not the full population-wide cost of POET’s search and unsuccessful branches**. The comparison tests whether a direct path can reproduce discoveries with a comparable ancestral training allowance.

Progress is measured by normalized Euclidean distance between the target parameter vector and the closest terrain the control solved. Components are divided by reference maxima and the norm by `√5`. The plotted normalization uses roughness/gap/stump maxima of **8/8/3**, different from the encoding table’s 10/10/5; these are analysis scales, not interchangeable limits. The distance does not directly measure performance on the final target or differences in terrain seed.

Controls often reach the lowest challenge tier but remain farther from very and extremely challenging targets. Figure 6’s median normalized distances are approximately **0.09, 0.23, and 0.37**; these are visual readings, not exact tabulated scores. Pairwise tier differences and distances from zero are reported significant at `p < 0.01`. Targets sharing a POET run and ancestry are not independent discoveries, limiting a naive interpretation of every target as a fresh replicate.

After creation, solved environments take mean **638±133, 1,180±343, and 2,178±368 outer iterations** across the three tiers, with reported 95% confidence intervals. These exclude the time spent discovering their ancestors and are not per-task total training cost. The results challenge this direct-path curriculum, not every possible curriculum algorithm.

## Experiment 3: transfers and the no-transfer ablation

A concrete backward-transfer case begins with a flat-ground solver that moves in a crouched posture. A child environment with stumps appears at iteration **400**. Its solver eventually learns to stand and clear the stumps; at iteration **1,175**, this behavior transfers back to flat ground. With a 3,000-iteration comparison, the crouched lineage reaches **309**, while the transferred standing lineage reaches **349**. A separate 3,000-iteration continuation without transfer preserves the poor gait. This supports the value of that transfer, but “stuck forever” is stronger than the finite experiment establishes.[^7]

Across the three full runs, there are **18,894 / 19,014 / 18,798 grouped replacement opportunities**, of which **53.62% / 49.26% / 48.89%** find a better solver. Each opportunity includes the incoming set of direct and proposal candidates, so these percentages are not success probabilities for a randomly selected individual source transfer.

The authors then run **three additional POET searches with transfer disabled**. To measure coverage, they sample 1,000 target environments in each challenge tier and compute distance to the nearest generated-and-solved environment, for 3,000 probes total. Lower nearest distance means better coverage. Full POET covers more of this parameter space, with a reported Mann–Whitney `p < 2.2×10^-16`. The large probe count does not turn three independent training runs into thousands of independent runs; correlated distances and training cost should be considered when interpreting that very small p-value.

No-transfer runs solve **no extremely challenging environments**. Figure 8 displays the relative difficulty composition of generated-and-solved sets, not the success rate on a fixed common set of tasks. Its caption says none were generated, while the main evidence is explicitly about generated-and-solved environments. The conservative conclusion concerns the solved repertoire. This ablation supports transfer’s contribution, but a fully matched accounting should also include the ES proposal computation removed with transfer.

## Diversity, open-endedness, and limitations

The final qualitative experiment displays terrain sections from one run: large gaps with high stumps and roughness, varied gap widths with modest other obstacles, or variable stumps with easy gaps. All three main runs generate and solve examples in every challenge tier. That establishes simultaneous diversity within a run, rather than just collecting unrelated successes from many isolated searches.[^7]

It does not establish a single general policy that solves the entire archive. POET maintains specialists, retires old active pairs, and does not report comprehensive backward-retention tests or unseen-seed robustness. Genetic novelty in terrain parameters can also differ from meaningful novelty in required behavior. Bounds on terrain, a fixed body, finite compute, and manually designed reward/criteria limit how far the process can continue.

The discussion proposes richer encodings, evolving bodies/controllers/environments together, changing reward functions, alternative inner optimizers, and meta-learning. These are future possibilities. The central demonstrated result is that coevolving challenges plus cross-branch transfer can discover solutions missed by the tested direct routes; it is not empirical evidence of indefinite self-improvement.[^8]

## Concrete Everdict evolution experiments

POET suggests separating **training-task invention** from **capability confirmation**. A driver could propose task variants around known weaknesses—different file layouts, interaction constraints, or edge cases—under a fixed task-generator contract. The platform should preserve the original task family, generator version, seed, expected behavior, and disclosure tier. A generated task needs a trustworthy success definition before its results become evidence.

Test three equal-budget curricula: a fixed task mixture, a single increasing-difficulty path, and a small branching curriculum with transfer. Keep candidate edit permissions and evaluation machinery constant. An environment variant that the candidate can already solve may still be useful for measuring regression; it need not remain an active learning niche. A variant that no current candidate solves can be exploratory, but should not be assumed learnable merely because its author says so.

For transfer, compare direct reuse of an immutable candidate with limited adaptation from that candidate, counting all proposal and selection costs. Preserve source and adapted digests separately. Retain neutral or specialized candidates as possible parents without letting success on a self-generated niche authorize activation. Record failed transfers as well as wins to measure the actual value of the archive.

Finally, test gains on **independent, platform-owned confirmation families**, plus previous capabilities. Harder generated tasks, more diverse environments, and more successful transfers are process measurements. The intended product outcome is better Everdict task performance under the declared cost and authority contract. Under the [first-loop proposal](../evolution-first-loop.md), the driver can explore curricula; it cannot alter confirmation budgets, disclosure, adoption rules, or the evidence ledger to accommodate its discoveries.
