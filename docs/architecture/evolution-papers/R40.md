# R40 — Meta Context Engineering: evolving the procedure that builds context

Meta Context Engineering (MCE) optimizes two different objects: a skill explaining **how to learn useful context**, and the context files and retrieval code produced by executing that skill. Frozen language models perform both searches. This makes MCE directly relevant to an Everdict driver that learns how to curate evidence, rather than merely appending more task advice. The pinned v2 experiments concern five mostly classification-oriented domains, not long terminal or software-engineering episodes.[^1]

## Background: context is a function, not just a paragraph

An ordinary prompt is constant across queries. Retrieval-augmented generation selects information from a fixed collection. More general context engineering can combine static files with query-dependent retrieval, filtering, formatting, and composition. MCE represents this as `c(x)`: the context returned for query `x` by a collection of resources and operators.

GEPA searches reflective prompt revisions; Dynamic Cheatsheet and ACE accumulate experience under predefined update workflows. MCE instead lets an agent write both the representation and the procedure that learns it. This expands the search space beyond a fixed list of advice entries. It does not make the system free of inductive bias: the models, initial prompts, one-shot interface, history selection, and validation objective still constrain search.[^2]

The analogy is to AutoML or meta-learning: context corresponds loosely to learned parameters, while the skill corresponds to an architecture and training algorithm. Here both are files or code, and the underlying model weights remain frozen. The equations express an optimization objective; the agents do not solve either level's global optimum exactly.

## The two-level loop and its persistent history

At each outer iteration, the meta-agent examines a history database containing every earlier skill, resulting context, training score, and validation score. It proposes a new skill by selectively combining or revising prior approaches. The paper calls this agentic crossover, but it need not mean a fixed two-parent genetic operation.[^3]

The base-agent receives this skill, the previous best context as a warm start, and training rollouts produced with that context. It analyzes successes and failures, edits context files, and implements the retrieval function. The exported candidate is then evaluated on training and validation data. All attempts enter history; the better of the candidate and incumbent supplies the next warm start.

The resulting search is a history-informed `(1+1)` evolution strategy: one new candidate competes with one incumbent, while proposal generation can inspect the entire archive. A rejected skill remains informative. Selection chooses the resulting context, so a skill's score also depends on its inherited context and the stochastic base-agent execution. It is not an isolated estimate of the learning procedure's reusable quality.

A skill may contain methodology, scripts, templates, validation instructions, and dynamic operators. The meta-agent writes only its designated skill area; the base-agent operates inside its iteration workspace. A prescribed callable interface is checked after execution. Such interface validation establishes that an artifact can be called, not that its advice generalizes or that its scoring semantics are correct.

## Concrete implementation and information available to the agents

The experiments use five optimization epochs and the Claude Agent SDK. MiniMax M2.1 performs agentic engineering; DeepSeek V3.1 performs ordinary task inference and reflection. AEGIS2 uses Qwen3-8B as its task model. Base-agents can additionally invoke DeepSeek V3.1 and `text-embedding-3-small` utilities. No model is trained during this procedure.[^4]

For comparability, deployment is restricted to one `query → context` retrieval call followed by task-model inference. The broader framework permits arbitrary context programs, but these experiments do not test an executor freely exploring a skill directory over a long tool-use trajectory.

The meta-agent sees full training data and aggregate training/validation scores. The base-agent sees training questions, predictions, labels, and correctness. This is supervised feedback when labels are available, not label-free self-improvement. Validation aggregates influence later proposals even without disclosing validation examples.

The utility listing limits one LLM batch to 100 prompts with concurrency fifty and retries up to three times. That is a per-batch limit, not a total campaign call budget. Exact total engineering tokens, retries, filesystem work, and embedding costs require additional accounting. MIPROv2 and GEPA use heavy configurations; GEPA's rollout budget is matched, and ACE runs up to five epochs subject to stopping conditions. Equal rollout budgets do not equate all agentic computation.

## Dataset construction and scoring scope

| Domain | Train / validation / test | Actual measured task |
|---|---|---|
| FiNER | 200 / 100 / 100 | Classify a specified financial entity's XBRL tag; accuracy |
| USPTO-50k | 50 / 30 / 100 | Predict precursor SMILES with reaction type supplied; exact match |
| Symptom2Disease | 200 / 50 / 212 | Choose one of 22 diagnosis labels; normalized accuracy |
| LawBench | 200 / 50 / 100 | Chinese criminal-charge prediction; micro-F1 |
| AEGIS2 | 400 / 128 / 140 | User-prompt safe/unsafe classification; unsafe-class F1 |

FiNER samples twelve debt and credit tag categories, while the prompt lists 139 possible tags. It is not full-document entity extraction over the entire original benchmark. USPTO stratifies across ten reaction types and supplies the type in the question, making it a conditioned retrosynthesis setting.[^5]

Symptom2Disease preserves the original 212-item test split. Its similar training and test descriptions favor matching examples; success on this dataset is not clinical validation. LawBench uses one subtask rather than the full legal suite. Its task-specification prompt describes exact set matching, whereas the reported metric is micro-F1 with partial credit; those objectives should not be conflated.

AEGIS2 selects four categories on which a Llama safeguard performs worst, then balances the test set with seventy unsafe and seventy safe examples. The training set is 300 unsafe and 100 safe. This is a selected hard-category distribution with a changed class balance, not population-wide guardrail performance. Risk-category explanations are secondary; the reported F1 evaluates binary prompt labels, despite broader category-classification language elsewhere in the paper.

## Offline results: strong gains, but relative averages need care

| Method | FiNER % | USPTO % | Symptoms % | Law F1 | Safety F1 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Base | 58.0 | 6.0 | 63.7 | 0.36 | 0.54 |
| ICL | 64.0 | 9.0 | 84.4 | 0.57 | 0.59 |
| MIPROv2 | 69.0 | 14.0 | 73.1 | 0.60 | 0.59 |
| GEPA | 66.0 | 15.0 | 70.8 | 0.69 | 0.76 |
| ACE | 71.0 | 18.0 | 79.2 | 0.65 | 0.68 |
| MCE | 75.0 | 20.0 | 89.2 | 0.70 | 0.80 |

MCE leads all five offline columns. However, the strongest alternative differs by task: ACE for finance and chemistry, ICL for symptoms, and GEPA for law and safety. The gains over those column-wise alternatives are 4, 2, and 4.8 percentage points, then 0.01 and 0.04 F1.[^6]

The reported 89.1% mean relative gain over the base is reproducible from the table, but chemistry's 6→20 contributes a 233.3% relative improvement to that mean. It is not an 89.1-point accuracy gain. The conclusion's broader 5.6–53.8% range and 16.9% average are not the direct mean improvement over each offline column's strongest baseline: that calculation is about 5.8%. The comparator and setting must accompany the headline.

The paper compares with specialized legal and safeguard models, reporting 0.70 versus 0.56 and 0.80 versus 0.72. These provide context, not proof that context learning replaces specialist training in general; dataset subsets, model versions, and training regimes differ. No repeated-search confidence intervals establish the statistical reliability of the small 0.01 LawBench gap.

## Online results: fixed skills and no skills trade places

Online evaluation processes test instances sequentially and scores each only on its first prediction. Context can learn from previously processed instances. This is an adaptive stream evaluation rather than a fixed-artifact test on untouched independent examples. Dataset order is held the same across methods.[^4]

The full evolving-meta-skill loop is not used online. The two variants are autonomous context engineering without a skill, and context engineering guided by a fixed task-specification-derived skill. Their results are respectively `67 / 18 / 76.9 / .70 / .68` and `68 / 20 / 76.4 / .66 / .63` across the five domains.[^6]

The fixed skill helps finance and chemistry but loses on symptoms, law, and safety. Its reported mean relative gain, 74.1%, exceeds the no-skill 71.3% because of the chosen normalization, particularly the low chemistry baseline. It would be misleading to say the fixed skill wins every domain or that online experiments demonstrate continuous meta-skill evolution. ACE online also falls below the no-context symptom baseline, 62.3 versus 63.7.

## Context size, training efficiency, and model transfer

On FiNER, a short MCE context of about 1.5K tokens reaches 73%, versus 65% for a similarly sized early ACE context. A longer MCE context reaches 75% at 20K tokens, whereas the final ACE context reaches 70% at 79K. The main ACE table uses its better 71% checkpoint. These comparisons involve different search stages, not a controlled experiment changing only length.[^7]

The paper reports contexts reaching 44K tokens for law and 86K for chemistry. Its law case study also describes a roughly 30KB final structure after shrinking an 88KB artifact. Tokens, bytes, and selected iteration artifacts differ; those figures should not be converted into one supposed final context length.

FiNER optimization takes 1.9 hours versus ACE's 25.8, a reported 13.6× speedup attributed to batch analysis and parallel reflection. MCE reaches the stated 95% training threshold by 450 rollouts; ACE peaks at 94% after 2,169. These are training trajectories, not 95% test accuracy, and rollout counts do not include every form of engineering work.

Transfer fixes contexts learned with DeepSeek V3.1 and applies them to Llama3.3-70B, Qwen3-8B, and Gemma3-4B. MCE exceeds ACE in all nine reported cells across FiNER, symptoms, and law, and shows smaller average degradation from the source model. Chemistry is excluded because the smaller models struggle with context length and valid answers; safety already uses a small model.

The extreme 172.6% mean gain for Gemma includes LawBench moving from 0.01 to 0.03 F1. That remains poor absolute performance. Transfer is stronger within these selected tasks; it does not establish cross-domain transfer of the **learning skill**, which is a different object.

## Ablations and the limits of causal attribution

FiNER offline scores are 73% without skills, 71% with a fixed skill, and 75% with evolving skills. Thus the measured incremental advantage of meta-level evolution over autonomous base-level engineering is two points, while much of the gain exists without any skill guidance.[^8]

Replacing ACE's reflector and curator with MiniMax M2.1 reduces final accuracy from 70% to 67% and grows its playbook from 79K to 114K tokens, terminating early at the context limit. This argues against a simple claim that MiniMax improves every workflow. It does not logically rule out all knowledge-transfer or model–workflow interaction effects in MCE. A fully crossed model-by-method experiment would be stronger.

The ablation is concentrated on one small finance test set. There is no broad isolation of crossover, history access, warm starts, representation changes, retrieval code, or separate compute allocations. Explanations that global restructuring and batch synthesis cause every gain remain plausible interpretations rather than independently established mechanisms.

## What actually evolved in the five case studies

Finance evolves from surface tag patterns toward semantic distinctions and error-driven decision chains. Chemistry combines successful transformations with failure warnings and procedural checks. Both ultimately use full-context concatenation in the described retrieval implementation; having a retrieval interface does not imply selective retrieval.[^9]

Symptoms evolves toward conservative changes, stopping rules, and a 1,440-line routing program. Law organizes errors by processing stage—fact comprehension, act extraction, charge selection, naming—and uses a 177-line augmentation function. Safety develops explicit false-positive and false-negative handling with a 995-line routing function. These cases show that the search can move complexity into code rather than eliminate it.

The printed artifacts also reveal limits. The proposed reusable skill retains iteration-specific references despite the system prompt forbidding them. Some task rules overgeneralize: numbered legal facts are treated as separate charges, and simple symptom strings trigger decisive labels. These are learned benchmark heuristics, not validated legal or clinical principles. The safety router's broad early-safe keyword rules likewise require adversarial and distribution-shift testing before reuse.

## Everdict proposal: evaluate the learner separately from its product

Everdict can treat the context-learning procedure as versioned driver advice while retaining the platform's frozen evaluator, disclosure limits, and adoption authority. The archive should connect `learning skill → inherited context → generated candidate → train/select/confirm evidence`. A strong candidate does not alone prove that its generating procedure is reliably better.

A first experiment should compare an autonomous curator, a fixed curated procedure, and an evolving procedure under equal total budgets. Use the same raw evidence and immutable artifact interface. Measure held-out completion, artifact size, retrieval cost, regression rate, rejected-proposal reuse, and the variability of rebuilding from the same procedure.

A second experiment should replay each proposed learning procedure from multiple identical starting contexts and on a different task family. This separates inherited good context from genuine learning-procedure transfer. Preserve rejected candidates in history, but keep adaptive selection evidence distinct from confirmation evidence. The meta-agent may choose how to analyze evidence; it must not redefine the evaluator or its access rights.

These are proposed Everdict changes, not shipped behavior or MCE's own tests. The most useful insight is to evolve the **method of learning** while measuring both the resulting artifact and the reproducibility of producing it.
