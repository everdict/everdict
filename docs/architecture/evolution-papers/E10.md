# E10 — Illuminating search spaces by mapping elites

## Reading guide and evidence scope

Mouret and Clune’s **1504.04909v1 (2015)** introduces **MAP-Elites**, the multi-dimensional archive of phenotypic elites. This is the foundational paper about the algorithm, not a modern LLM implementation. The complete text, methods, and principal result/robot figures were reviewed. Its experiments are explicitly preliminary, with incomplete runs and conflicting budget descriptions identified below. No experiment was independently reproduced.[^1]

For Everdict, the important idea is that a candidate can be worth retaining because it is the best **of a particular kind**, even if it is not the global winner. That creates more possible parents for future search. It does not imply that every retained candidate should be deployed or that filling an archive proves improvement on user tasks.

## The problem: finding one winner versus mapping what is possible

Ordinary optimization seeks a high-scoring solution. Multi-objective optimization seeks trade-offs among objectives, often retaining a Pareto frontier. An **illumination algorithm** asks a different question: what is the best known solution in each region of a chosen feature space? A slow, heavy robot may be dominated by a faster, lighter robot under a speed/weight objective, yet it can still be useful to know the fastest achievable design among heavy robots.[^2]

Distinguish three objects. The **genotype** `x` is the editable description, such as a network or robot blueprint. The **phenotype** is what that description produces. The **feature descriptor** `b(x)` summarizes selected measurable properties of the phenotype or its behavior. A separate scalar **fitness** `f(x)` measures quality. The genotype can be high dimensional even when the displayed feature map has only two axes.

The user chooses the descriptor dimensions and discretizes them into cells. Features need not be objectives to maximize. Height, material proportion, or a style of behavior can be coordinates without implying that more is better. Multiple, possibly infinitely many genotypes may land in one cell. Conversely, some cells are physically impossible or simply never reached. An empty cell is not proof of impossibility.

This matters in deceptive search spaces: improving the current scalar score may lead into a local optimum, while reaching a better solution may require passing through candidates of lower overall quality. Keeping useful candidates in other niches can preserve such stepping stones.

## The algorithm and the actual acceptance rule

Initialize an empty map and evaluate random genotypes. For each one, measure its descriptor and fitness, locate its cell, and retain it if the cell is empty or it beats the current occupant. Once initialized, repeatedly sample **uniformly from occupied cells**, mutate the selected elite, evaluate the child, and apply the same local replacement rule.[^3]

```text
archive = empty map
for each evaluation within the budget:
    child = random candidate during initialization
            otherwise mutate(a uniformly sampled occupied-cell elite)
    descriptor, score = evaluate(child)
    cell = discretize(descriptor)
    if cell is empty or score > archive[cell].score:
        archive[cell] = child and its measured score
return archive
```

The child can land in a different cell from its parent. It competes with the incumbent of the **destination** cell, not necessarily its parent or the global champion. A low-scoring candidate can therefore enter an empty niche without weakening the best recorded score elsewhere. A candidate that is worse than its own cell’s incumbent is discarded by the simple algorithm. Thus MAP-Elites preserves diversity across chosen cells, not every neutral mutation or every genetically different program.

The pseudocode uses strict improvement; an adjacent comment loosely describes a non-strict comparison, so exact tie handling should be pinned in an implementation. This does not change the core local-competition idea. The paper permits mutation and/or crossover conceptually, but the soft-robot implementation does not use crossover or NEAT-style speciation.

All reported experiments use a batched implementation, with coarse-to-fine cell subdivision where applicable. Subdivision encourages broad early exploration and finer later resolution. It does not manufacture forgotten candidates for newly separated cells: retaining only one coarse-cell elite can lose other potentially useful genotypes. The paper proposes alternatives such as multiple occupants, biased parent selection, and geographically restricted crossover as future work, not established improvements.

## Related algorithms and why the difference matters

**Novelty Search** rewards being far from previously observed behavior, rather than high scalar fitness. **Novelty Search plus Local Competition (NS+LC)** adds performance relative to nearby individuals. Its novelty and local-performance scores then participate in population-level selection; neighborhood relationships change as the population/archive changes.

**MOLE**, Multi-Objective Landscape Exploration, combines global fitness and behavioral diversity. An incremental improvement in a crowded, medium-quality region may be neither globally competitive nor novel enough to survive. MAP-Elites instead explicitly protects the best occupant of that region.[^4]

The MAP-Elites archive serves as both population and memory of occupied niches. Local replacement uses a direct cell lookup, avoiding the repeated neighborhood computations of a novelty-based method. This is a simpler bookkeeping operation, not a guarantee of constant total search cost: evaluating the candidate, computing descriptors, storing artifacts, and managing a large map can dominate.

An ordinary optimizer can still produce diverse intermediate candidates. The experiments therefore record **all evaluated baseline candidates** and retrospectively keep each cell’s best, rather than comparing MAP-Elites’ full history with only the baseline’s final winner. This makes the illumination comparison more informative.

## Four metrics, with a worked interpretation

Let `M(c)` be the best score found in cell `c` by **any run of any compared method**, and let `U` be the union of cells any method reached. These are empirical reference values, not known true optima. Let `m(c)` be a particular run’s best score, assigning zero to unfilled cells for the reliability calculation.[^5]

| Metric | Definition and question |
| --- | --- |
| Global performance | Best score anywhere in the run, normalized by the best known overall score: did it find a strong single winner? |
| Global reliability | Mean `m(c)/M(c)` over `U`: did it find good solutions throughout the empirically reachable map? |
| Precision, or opt-in reliability | Mean `m(c)/M(c)` over only the run’s occupied cells: how good are the solutions where it chose to provide one? |
| Coverage | Number of cells the run filled divided by `|U|`: how much of the observed feature space did it reach? |

For example, if ten reference cells exist and a run fills two at their reference best, coverage is 0.2, precision 1.0, and reliability 0.2. Another run filling eight cells at 90% of reference quality has coverage 0.8, precision 0.9, and reliability 0.72. The second is a much better map despite lower precision. Under matching nonzero normalization assumptions, reliability equals coverage times precision.

These ratios require care with zero or negative scores. The original formulas also occasionally describe nonzero entries as filled cells, which is unsuitable if valid zero-score candidates can occupy a cell. In Everdict, occupancy should be explicit. The reference map is built from the same compared searches, so a normalized score of 1 means matching the best **observed**, not proving global optimality. Adding a stronger method can change the denominator and reduce older normalized scores without changing their actual behavior.

## Experiment 1: neural networks on an eight-pixel retina

The task presents every one of **256 binary input patterns** to a neural network. The network decides whether a specified object pattern appears on both halves of an eight-pixel retina. Fitness is the fraction of correct classifications. The feature axes are **connection cost**, defined through squared connection lengths, and **network modularity**, approximating Newman’s modularity score. The experiment maps performance across network organization; it is not a held-out machine-learning generalization test.[^6]

Twenty independent random-seed runs compare MAP-Elites, a traditional fitness-only evolutionary algorithm, NS+LC, and random sampling under reportedly equal evaluation budgets. Baseline maps include all visited candidates. MAP-Elites is significantly better on **all four metrics**, with the paper reporting two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests at `p < 10^-7`. The boxplots show strong map coverage and precision as well as the best single-solution result. These findings are specific to the tested task and parameter settings; the paper itself says the surprising random-sampling advantage over NS+LC coverage needs broader parameter testing.

A claimed comparison with MOLE is only anecdotal in this version. Earlier MOLE figures combine 30 runs at another resolution, so the authors explicitly defer a matched quantitative comparison. It must not be counted as an additional controlled baseline win.

The budget description is inconsistent. Section 6.1 says **10,000 evaluations**, whereas Methods lists **10,000 iterations, batch size 2,000, and initial batch 20,000**. If each iteration evaluates a full batch, that implies roughly 20 million additional evaluations, not ten thousand. The map is described as starting at 16×16 but changing to 64×64 at iteration 0, then 128×128 at 1,250, 256×256 at 2,500, and 512×512 at 5,000. Exact run configuration is needed before quoting sample efficiency or reproducing the schedule.

The lineage analysis adds a mechanism hypothesis. Most immediate parent–child moves stay relatively nearby in feature space, but complete lineages of four sampled final elites travel through distant regions. Some successful children originate from moderately distant, not immediately neighboring, niches. This supports the value of retaining distributed stepping stones. It is not a randomized ablation proving that each ancestor or cross-region transition was necessary.

## Experiment 2: simulated soft robots

Robots occupy a **10×10×10 voxel** workspace. A voxel is empty or contains stiff bone-like material, soft support tissue, or one of two muscle materials expanding and contracting in opposite phases. A **CPPN**, a network with geometric activation functions such as sine or Gaussian, maps voxel coordinates and distance from the center to occupancy and material. This indirect encoding encourages regular shapes. The CPPN itself evolves through a NEAT-inspired Sferes implementation without crossover or speciation. Disconnected material is reduced to the central connected patch.[^7]

Fitness is distance traveled in **10 simulated seconds** in Voxcad. The feature map spans body occupancy and bone proportion, ending at **128×128**, from 64×64, with initial batch 4,096, batch size 1,024, and 1,400 iterations. The text initially names the axes in one order, while the illustrated maps show **filled-voxel proportion horizontally and bone proportion vertically**; follow the actual labels.

Controls are fitness-only EA and EA plus a diversity objective, implemented using NSGA-II. Diversity is average descriptor distance to the other population members. Equal evaluation counts are reported per treatment, but only completed runs appear: **7 EA, 5 EA+diversity, and 8 MAP-Elites**, although ten were launched for each. Completion-based inclusion and unequal replicate counts are limitations of this preliminary draft.

MAP-Elites has much higher **reliability and coverage** (`p < 0.002`). However, its better median **global performance is not statistically significant** (`p > 0.05`), and its **precision is worse** than both controls (`p < 0.01`). The interpretation follows the metric example: the controls concentrate on a few niches, whereas MAP-Elites spreads its budget widely. The authors hypothesize that more computation could improve its precision; that is not a measured result here.

The maps reveal families of designs varying smoothly in body size and bone proportion, including faster flexible forms and slower heavily supported forms. They also expose an anomalously fast band of extremely thin, one-voxel-wide bodies attributed to a simulator quirk. This is valuable evidence that illumination can reveal an evaluator artifact. It is not evidence those bodies would locomote as well in the physical world. The simulated robots are not fabricated in this experiment.

## Experiment 3: a real compliant robot arm

The physical arm has three servo joints connected by flexible tubes. A camera tracks a red marker at its tip. A candidate is three joint-angle commands, each constrained to a specified range. The **single feature dimension is horizontal tip position**, discretized into **64 cells**; fitness is maximum vertical tip position. The result approximates the upper boundary of the arm’s reachable workspace despite complicated flexible-body physics.[^8]

MAP-Elites is compared with random angle sampling and a regular grid in **joint-angle space**. This is different from gridding the resulting tip-position space: many joint combinations produce similar positions, while some useful positions are hard to reach. MAP-Elites and random sampling have ten repetitions; the deterministic grid command sequence is run once. A deterministic sequence does not eliminate physical measurement noise.

All methods find similar boundaries in the easier region around camera x=600–800, where the arm can rest part of itself on the table. MAP-Elites gives better coverage and heights in intermediate and lower x regions requiring less common joint combinations. The authors explicitly call the evidence too preliminary for reliable statistical conclusions.

The budgets conflict substantially. The main text states **640 evaluations** for MAP-Elites/random and **729=9³** for grid search, while also describing eight grid points per joint. Methods instead gives **420 evaluations** for MAP-Elites/random and **512=8³** for the grid. Its MAP-Elites schedule of 120 initial evaluations plus 30 batches of 10 agrees with 420. Report both descriptions rather than silently choosing one and claiming a precise equal-budget superiority.

## What the paper establishes, and what it does not

The paper supplies a simple mechanism for preserving high quality across predefined niches, with strong neural-task results, broader simulated-robot coverage, and a preliminary physical demonstration. It does not prove that all niches are reachable, that retained elites are globally optimal, or that more diversity always improves the best final score. Descriptor choice, bin resolution, mutation locality, evaluation noise, and total budget determine whether the archive is useful.

Most importantly, the authors explicitly state that fixed-feature MAP-Elites **cannot itself exhibit open-ended evolution**, because it cannot invent new kinds of niches outside the predefined feature space. Refining existing cells is not the same as creating a new behavioral dimension. [POET](E11.md) addresses part of this limitation by evolving environments; [AlphaEvolve](E07.md) uses archive ideas within LLM-driven program search, and [DGM](E06.md) retains agent lineages with different selection mechanics. These are related systems, not identical algorithms.[^9]

## Concrete Everdict evolution experiments

Treat MAP-Elites as a **driver search policy**, not a platform adoption rule. The current [first-loop proposal](../evolution-first-loop.md) permits driver-owned ranking and curation while preserving platform-owned validity, immutable candidate artifacts, disclosure, and confirmation accounting.

Begin with a small measurable descriptor space, perhaps selection-set tool-call budget versus observed task-family strengths, while keeping success as the fitness measure. A descriptor must come from declared measurements or inspectable candidate properties, not the candidate’s own claim to be “novel.” Avoid many axes: ten bins on each of six dimensions already create a million potential cells and dilute a small evaluation budget.

Compare champion-only search, a simple unstructured archive, and a small cell-based archive with identical initial candidates, proposal model, allowed edits, and evaluation budget. Report best independently confirmed capability, per-family regressions, archive coverage, occupied-cell quality, and cost. More filled cells without better useful candidates is an illumination result, not automatically a product improvement.

Store immutable parent/child digests and retain original evaluation records even when an elite is replaced. Reevaluate promising occupants sufficiently to avoid a lucky noisy score becoming permanent authority. Use selection evidence for niche assignment and confirmation only under the campaign’s frozen accounting rules. A candidate may be an eligible parent while still being ineligible for activation.

Finally, distinguish two hypotheses: diverse parents help search discover a better common agent, or different specialists should be routed at execution time. The latter adds a router and its own evaluation problem. MAP-Elites motivates both possibilities, but its archive alone does not validate runtime routing or authorize a new skill/campaign subject in Everdict.
