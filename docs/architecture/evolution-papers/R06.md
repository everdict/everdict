# R06 — SkillRet: retrieving the right skills

SkillRet studies how to retrieve useful procedural documents from a large agent-skill library, particularly when a request needs several skills. It contributes a public benchmark, task-specific embedding and reranking models, and diagnostic analyses. The inspected v3 includes a functional-equivalence audit and a small downstream Terminal-Bench study, which are important additions beyond the abstract-level description.[^1]

## Problem and prerequisite concepts

A large catalogue cannot fit in the agent's prompt. Retrieval must select a few relevant skills from a request that may include long background material and several distinct needs. Matching topical words is insufficient: a document about testing software may discuss tests without providing the specific procedure the request needs.

An embedding retriever maps queries and skill documents into vectors and ranks them by similarity. A reranker then jointly examines each query–document pair among the first stage's top candidates. The first stage controls which skills are even considered; the second controls their order. No reranker can recover an omitted skill unless the candidate set is expanded. This division motivates separate ranking and coverage metrics.[^1]

## Corpus construction and why the counts differ

The raw crawl contains 22,795 marketplace listings. Five filtering stages recover usable descriptions, filter language, filter declared licenses and related duplicates, deduplicate normalized bodies, and deduplicate name–description pairs. This leaves **17,810 curated documents**. Query eligibility and the later equivalence audit reduce the final benchmark to **16,129 skills**, divided into 10,123 training and 6,006 evaluation skills. These numbers describe different stages, not interchangeable corpus sizes.[^2]

The collection has six major categories and eighteen subcategories, dominated by software engineering. English filtering removes documents with more than 3% non-Latin characters, so it is not a multilingual retrieval benchmark. Exact hashes remove identical text but cannot detect differently worded procedures accomplishing the same task.

## Query generation and label correction

For each synthetic request, the generator is shown the names and descriptions of one, two, or three sampled skills and asked to create a realistic task requiring them without mentioning skill names. GAIA questions provide style examples. Inverse-frequency sampling improves skill coverage. Training queries use Qwen3.5-122B-A10B; evaluation queries use Claude Opus 4.6, reducing reliance on one generator's style. The final release has 63,259 training queries and 4,392 evaluation queries.[^2]

Filtering rejects excessive query/document 3-gram overlap and uses three review perspectives: skill coherence, query quality, and discriminability. Two invalid judgments reject a query; one sends it to human review. Three expert annotators provide the final validation stage. This is a substantial quality process, but synthetic labels still encode judgments about which skills are necessary rather than observed production usage.

The functional audit retrieves five neighbors per evaluation skill using e5-mistral-7b-instruct, retains pairs with similarity at least 0.80, and uses GPT-5.5 to judge substitutability from full bodies. Equivalent evaluation skills are grouped and their relevance labels remapped to a representative. Detected training/evaluation capability overlaps are removed, including queries depending on them. Thus a functionally correct alternative is less likely to be scored as a false negative. The audit only removes **detected** overlaps; neighbor-limited model judgments cannot certify that every duplicate has been found.[^2]

## Training and evaluation protocol

The study compares nineteen first-stage systems, including BM25, encoder models, and decoder embeddings from below 100 million to 12 billion parameters. Documents include name, description, and body up to each model's supported limit. A top-20 reranking depth is selected after comparing 10, 20, and 50. Model-specific prompting and unequal maximum lengths remain relevant when interpreting model comparisons.[^3]

SkillRet embeddings fine-tune Qwen3-Embedding at 0.6B and 8B using 127,190 positive query–skill pairs with MultipleNegativesRankingLoss: a positive pair is pulled together while other batch documents provide contrastive negatives. The 0.6B reranker trains a yes/no relevance decision using binary cross-entropy and hard negatives mined by the trained retriever. Evaluations use one NVIDIA B200 per model.[^3]

| Metric | Meaning | Example of the distinction |
|---|---|---|
| NDCG@10 | Ranking quality, rewarding relevant skills placed earlier | Moving a needed skill from rank 10 to rank 1 helps |
| Recall@10 | Fraction of required skills appearing in the top ten | Finding two of three gives partial credit |
| Completeness@10 | Fraction of queries for which all required skills are found | Two of three is incomplete |

For a request requiring data extraction, statistical analysis, and report generation, high average recall can hide failure to supply one essential stage. Completeness tests this bottleneck. The example is explanatory; it is not a separate experimental query from the paper.

## Main results

Table 3 reports the corrected evaluation set. The strongest off-the-shelf model by NDCG@10 is Harrier-OSS-0.6B at 70.26. SkillRouter-Embedding-0.6B reaches 73.54, SkillRet-Embedding-0.6B reaches 81.12, and SkillRet-Embedding-8B reaches 86.44. The latter exceeds the strongest prior retriever by 12.90 points and the strongest off-the-shelf retriever by 16.18 points. These are ranking-score points, not task-success percentage points.[^3]

The multi-skill analysis is especially useful. For queries needing three skills, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B has Completeness@10 of 1.8%, while its SkillRet-trained counterpart reaches 42.1%. For two skills, the values are 25.8% and 74.2%; for one, 83.8% and 92.1%. Fine-tuning therefore addresses a composition bottleneck that a single-skill hit rate can obscure, while the three-skill condition remains far from solved.[^4]

## Ablations and downstream utility

The document-representation ablation favors full bodies over names and descriptions; for Qwen3-Embedding-8B, pre-audit NDCG@10 rises from 48.60 to 60.00. Deeper reranking improves coverage but costs more. Injecting missing gold skills into the top-20 set provides an oracle diagnostic: with the SkillRet reranker, NDCG@10 rises from 87.74 to 88.89. The remaining gap includes ordering errors, not just first-stage omissions.[^4]

Sentence masking shows that removing the most important snippets sharply reduces performance, consistent with learning to focus on skill-relevant parts of noisy requests. It does not prove a unique internal attention mechanism. Further analyses cover category differences, graded relevance, false negatives among functionally equivalent skills, latency, and the weak correspondence between generic MTEB rankings and skill-retrieval rankings. Several appendix tables are explicitly **pre-audit** and should not be merged into the corrected leaderboard.[^4]

The downstream study runs 80 Terminal-Bench tasks over five trials with Claude Code and Opus 4.6. A prompt hook injects the top three bodies when similarity is at least 0.30, searching a pre-audit pool of 16,783 skills. Pass@1 is 65.5% without retrieval and 65.8% with it; mean reported cost per trial is $0.86 versus $0.78. This supports nearly unchanged observed success with lower measured cost, not a demonstrated large accuracy gain or formal non-inferiority result.[^4]

## Related work and limitations

SkillRouter is the nearest routing comparison; SkillsBench and studies of skills in the wild measure downstream usefulness; Skill Retrieval Augmentation separates retrieval, incorporation, and execution. SkillRet supplies an isolated training/evaluation problem needed to diagnose the first stage. The paper's comparison of earlier benchmarks refers to the versions it cites, so their counts can differ from newer versions covered elsewhere in this collection.

The v3 limitations section says downstream performance is not measured, while Section 5.3 and Table 6 do include the small Terminal-Bench experiment. Read that limitation as applying to the benchmark's primary retrieval objective, while recognizing the textual inconsistency. Production queries may be shorter, more ambiguous, conversational, or multilingual. Better ranking does not guarantee execution utility, and the small downstream study does not resolve that broader issue.[^1][^4]

## Everdict changes and an evaluation plan

Evolve a skill's retrieval representation alongside its procedural body, while giving each representation and corpus snapshot an identity. Compare metadata-only, full-body, and distilled-description retrieval. Include no-skill-needed requests, functional alternatives, and queries requiring multiple capabilities. Separate skill identity overlap from capability overlap across development and confirmation pools.

Measure ranking, completeness, loading, actual execution, and final task utility as distinct stages. A cheaper retriever with slightly lower NDCG may be preferable if the executor compensates; an excellent retriever can still inject distracting instructions. Test top-k and similarity thresholds under fixed context and latency budgets, then confirm the resulting agent candidate on independent tasks. These are proposed Everdict experiments, not results claimed for the repository.
